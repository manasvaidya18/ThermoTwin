Understand the problem

In plain words

Interviews and tests rarely show how someone handles real work: a production incident, a code review, an unclear requirement from a client, a difficult conversation with a teammate. Students seldom get to practise these situations before their first job. Build an AI-powered simulator in which people practise realistic workplace scenarios with AI characters and receive fair, evidence-based feedback afterwards.

What the PDF requires

  • Realistic scenarios with AI characters (manager, client, teammate), through chat or voice.
  • Scenarios that react to what the person says and does instead of following a fixed script.
  • A debrief at the end graded against a clear rubric that quotes moments from the conversation as evidence.
  • Optional: generate new scenarios from a job description; track progress over several attempts.
  • Outcomes: two or three complete, realistic scenarios; a rubric-based debrief with every point backed by a moment from the session; feedback a student would find genuinely useful.
  • Stage 1: roles and scenarios, rubric, conversation design, how feedback will be made fair and specific. Stage 2: one complete text scenario from start to debrief. Stage 3: voice or video interaction, several scenarios, scenario generation from a job description, progress tracking.

Our concept: DayOne

1. Director, actors, assessor

Three roles. The Director owns the hidden world state and decides what happens next. Actors voice characters within what they know. The Assessor silently tags behaviours to the rubric with turn ids while you play.

2. Evidence you can check

Every debrief point quotes your words. A program checks each quote exists in the transcript. Each dimension also shows "a stronger move would have been..." and "missed opportunities" drawn from beats you did not trigger.

3. Rewind and retry

The world state is stored per turn. Go back to the moment it went wrong, try a different approach, and compare the two branches in the debrief.

Obvious approachWhat goes wrongDayOne
One prompt: "act as my manager"No state, no rubric, no fairness.Scenario spec, state machine, assessor.

Why we win

It is the most emotionally engaging problem and our design makes it credible: verifiable quotes, fair seeded worlds, scenarios tested automatically by simulated candidates. Voice and rewind make a great demo.

Why we might lose

Crowded and hard to prove objectively. Without quote verification and the fairness story, it looks like a roleplay chatbot. Latency and character consistency are the technical risks, so test them early.

How it works inside

1. Scenario spec

id: incident_checkout_down
role: backend_engineer_intern
setting: "2:07 AM. You are on call for the first time. The checkout service is failing."
world_facts:                        # known to everyone
  - checkout error rate jumped to 40% at 01:58
hidden_facts:                       # unlocked by triggers
  - id: deploy      text: "A deploy at 01:50 reduced the DB connection pool from 50 to 5"        unlock: [asked_recent_changes]
  - id: cdn_alert   text: "A CDN latency alert is a red herring"
characters:
  - id: priya   role: engineering_manager  goal: "get status and an ETA every 10 minutes"  style: "anxious, brief"
                knows: [world_facts, customer_impact]
  - id: arjun   role: senior_on_call       goal: "help without taking over"              style: "calm, slow replies"
                knows: [world_facts, deploy]   reveal: "only if asked a specific question"
artefacts: [ dashboard_chart, error_log_snippet, deploy_list ]
beats:
  - id: b1  trigger: "turn >= 3 and not status_update_sent"  action: priya.pings("Any update? Customers are complaining.")  meter: patience -1
  - id: b2  trigger: "asked_recent_changes"                    action: reveal(deploy)
  - id: b3  trigger: "proposed_rollback and not assessed_risk" action: priya.asks("What is the risk if we roll back?")
timers: [ { at_turn: 6, event: "pager_alert: payment provider timeouts" } ]
rubric: [ prioritisation, communication, technical_judgement, ownership ]
success_paths: [ [asked_recent_changes, proposed_rollback, sent_status_update, scheduled_postmortem] ]

2. Turn loop

  1. The user message arrives (typed or transcribed).
  2. Assessor (asynchronous) tags observable behaviours: asked a clarifying question, gave a status update with ETA, escalated appropriately, proposed rollback, blamed someone, stayed silent too long. Each tag carries the turn id and the span of text.
  3. Director updates flags and meters, evaluates beat triggers, fires timed events, and chooses which character responds.
  4. Actor generates the reply from its persona, current emotion, knowledge list and the flags that unlocked facts. A checker verifies the reply does not mention facts the character should not know and does not break character.
  5. The state snapshot is stored for this turn (enabling rewind).

3. Fairness through seeded worlds

For assessment use, fix the random seed and the beat schedule so every candidate faces the same facts, characters and events. Actor wording may vary, but the hidden facts and triggers are identical. Practice mode may add randomness for variety. This is the difference between a practice toy and a fair evaluation.

4. Rubric

DimensionAnchors (0 to 3)Observable behaviours
Prioritisation0 chases side issues; 3 isolates the biggest customer impact first and sequences actions.Asks about impact; mitigates before investigating root cause.
Communication0 vague or silent; 3 concise, regular updates with ETA and next step.Status updates; tailors message to the audience.
Technical judgement0 guesses; 3 forms and tests hypotheses, weighs risk of rollback.Checks recent changes; asks for logs; considers risk.
Ownership0 deflects; 3 takes responsibility and plans follow-up.Says "I will"; proposes postmortem.
Collaboration and empathy0 dismissive; 3 supportive and clear.Acknowledges feelings; gives specific feedback.
Handling ambiguity0 assumes; 3 asks targeted questions and confirms understanding.Clarifying questions; restates requirements.

Each scenario uses a subset of four dimensions. Each level has a short example transcript for good, average and weak behaviour, used as calibration examples in the assessor prompt.

5. The debrief

Dimension: Communication            Score: 2 / 3         Confidence: high (two assessor passes agree)
Evidence (+): turn 9   "Rollback started at 02:21, I expect checkout to recover within 10 minutes. Next update at 02:31."
Evidence (-): turns 2 to 6  no update to Priya for 5 turns after she asked (her patience meter fell from 3 to 1)
A stronger move: after finding the pool change (turn 5), send a one-line update immediately, then continue investigating.
Missed opportunity: you never asked Arjun about recent deploys until turn 7; asking at turn 2 would have unlocked the cause earlier.
Next time try: use the pattern "what I know, what I am doing, when you will hear from me".
  • Quote verifier. Every quoted string must be an exact substring of the transcript at the cited turn. Failing items are dropped and regenerated once.
  • Two assessor passes with different prompts. Agreement is shown; disagreements lower confidence and are flagged.
  • Missed opportunities come from beats the Director did not fire and hidden facts that stayed locked, so they are factual, not invented.
  • Fairness controls. Accent and grammar are not scored unless the dimension is communication clarity; Hinglish and non-native phrasing are accepted; the dispute button highlights the evidence and requests a re-read.

6. Scenario generation from a job description

  1. Extract role, seniority, tools and soft-skill expectations from the job description.
  2. Pick templates from the library: incident, code review, ambiguous request, teammate conflict, estimation and pushback, mentoring, stakeholder update.
  3. Instantiate: company context, systems and names; write hidden facts and beats; choose rubric dimensions.
  4. Auto-validate: every dimension has at least two observable opportunities; at least one success path exists; no contradictions between facts; reasonable length.
  5. Simulation test: run the scenario with three simulated candidates (good, average, weak personas, played by an LLM). The scenario passes only if the scores separate (good above average above weak by at least one level) and hidden facts do not leak. This quality gate is a strong engineering idea.

7. Voice

  • Speech to text through the browser's Web Speech API (quick) or Whisper (more accurate); text to speech through the browser or a TTS API with different voices per character.
  • Latency budget about 2 to 3 seconds round trip: stream tokens, start speaking on the first sentence, keep replies short.
  • Show live captions and keep a text fallback.
  • Optional delivery indicators (pace, filler words) shown separately and never mixed into the rubric score.

8. Progress and replay

  • Store attempts with scores, evidence and timestamps. Show a radar over attempts, per-dimension trend, streaks.
  • Replay a session, or compare attempt 1 and attempt 3 on the same scenario with the same seed.
  • Rewind and branch. Jump to any turn, restore its stored state, play a different line, and compare branches.

Screens and flows

  • Scenario picker. Cards with difficulty, skills practised, time; a "Generate from job description" option.
  • Briefing. Setting and goal, without hidden facts; "start" begins the clock.
  • Simulation. Chat or voice; character avatars and names; side panel with artefacts; meters; timer; rewind button.
  • Debrief. Overall summary; rubric cards with score, quotes (click to jump to the turn), better move, missed opportunities, next-time tips; confidence and dispute button.
  • Progress. Attempts, radar, trend, compare two attempts.
  • Author view. Spec editor with validation results and the simulated-candidate test report.

Pitch kit

30-second pitch

The hardest parts of a first job are the ones nobody practises: an outage at 2 AM, feedback on a teammate's code, a client who cannot say what they want. DayOne lets students rehearse these with AI characters who react to what they say, then gives a debrief that quotes their own words against a clear rubric, shows what they missed and lets them rewind and try again. Scenarios are fair, seeded and automatically tested, and can be generated from any job description.

5-minute demo script

  1. 0:00 Problem. "Nobody practises the 2 AM page."
  2. 0:40 Start. Briefing; meters at 3.
  3. 1:15 Play. Ask Arjun about recent deploys; the pool change unlocks; Priya pings for an update; the meter drops, then recovers after a good status message.
  4. 2:30 Voice moment. Switch to voice for a status update.
  5. 3:00 Debrief. Rubric cards; click a quote and jump to the turn; read a missed opportunity.
  6. 3:40 Rewind. Jump to turn 3, ask the right question earlier, compare branches.
  7. 4:15 Generator. Paste a job description, show the scenario and the simulated-candidate validation report.
  8. 4:50 Close. Progress radar and roadmap.

Likely judge questions

How do you stop the AI from just improvising?

A Director holds the hidden state and beats; actors only know what their character knows and cannot reveal locked facts.

How do you know the feedback is fair?

Anchored rubric, two assessor passes with agreement shown, every point backed by a verified quote, seeded worlds in assessment mode, and a dispute option.

Can feedback be fabricated?

No quote is accepted unless it is an exact transcript substring; unsupported items are dropped.

How are new scenarios checked?

Structure checks, plus a simulation with good, average and weak candidates; the scenario passes only if scores separate and nothing leaks.

Is it a hiring tool?

Primarily practice and feedback. Employer mode needs more validation and we say so.

What about accents and language?

Grammar and accent are not scored unless the dimension is communication clarity; multilingual input is accepted.
