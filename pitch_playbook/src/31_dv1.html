Understand the problem

In plain words

A P&ID (piping and instrumentation diagram) is the engineering drawing of a process plant: tanks, pumps, heat exchangers, hundreds of pipes with bends, crossings and valves, and instruments attached everywhere. It represents the plant as built, so it is cluttered. The operators' screens in the control room need a simplified picture of the same process: only major equipment (tanks, vessels, pumps, exchangers) and the instruments listed in the IO list (the instruments the control system actually reads), connected in the right flow order, drawn with straight lines and no crossings. Today an engineer marks up each P&ID by hand, traces the flow and redraws it. Every drawing is different, so it is slow and error-prone.

What the PDF requires

  • Input: customer P&ID PDF and IO list Excel. Output: a simplified sketch as PDF, PPT or image showing major equipment and all IO-list instruments with their tags, connected in correct flow order with correct direction.
  • A tag-check report: IO-list tags found versus not found on the P&ID.
  • A good output is complete, accurate (same connections and flow direction), smart (duty and standby shown as parallel lines), readable (left to right, minimal bends, no crossovers) and generic (works on a P&ID it has never seen).
  • The tool must create the sketch itself; declare any third-party tools, APIs or models; sample data is for hackathon use only and must not be shared.
  • Example in the PDF: 18 instruments drawn, 14 in the IO list; duty and standby pumps (P-101A/B), each with a PT and FT, crossing; the sketch shows two parallel lines.

Our concept: Untangle Graph

1. Drawing becomes a graph first

Everything is built on a correct connectivity graph with flow direction. Layout is then a pure graph-drawing problem. We never "trace and copy lines".

2. Series-parallel reasoning

Duty and standby pumps are a parallel composition of identical branches. Detecting that structure lets the layout draw them as parallel rows, which solves the "pumps cross each other" case by construction.

3. Trace-back and confidence

Click a line in the sketch and the original path lights up on the P&ID. Low-confidence elements are flagged. Trust comes from showing the source.

How it works inside

1. Ingestion: three kinds of PDF

CaseHow to recognise itStrategy
A. Vector with a text layerMany path operators; words extractable.Best case. Take geometry and text with coordinates directly. Symbol recognition from primitives (circles, polylines).
B. Vector, text drawn as outlinesMany paths; no extractable words.Geometry from vectors, text from OCR on rendered crops.
C. Raster scanPage is an image.Render at 300 dpi (tiles for A0 or A1 sheets with 20% overlap), OCR, line segment detection, skeletonisation.

Always keep a rendered image for visual overlays and for any vision-model step. Remember the PDF says sample files are not to be shared; keep them local.

2. Symbols and tags

  • Instrument bubbles. Circles with text inside (and sometimes a horizontal bar). In vector input, find circles of the bubble size; in raster input use Hough circles. Read the text into a tag such as PT-101A.
  • Equipment. Large closed shapes with a label nearby. The label's letter prefix gives the type (T or V tank or vessel, P pump, E exchanger), so shape recognition only needs to find ports. This shortcut makes the system robust to symbol-style differences between customers.
  • Valves. Bow-tie symbols on lines; control valves have an actuator on top; check valves show direction. Most valves are not in the IO list and will be contracted away, but their direction cues feed the flow solver.
  • Tag normalisation. Uppercase, strip spaces and dashes, map common OCR confusions (O and 0, I and 1, S and 5), then fuzzy-match to the IO list under the ISA grammar. Record exact, normalised, fuzzy or not found, with distance and confidence.
  • Optional learning. Where a model helps (symbol classification of crops, reading difficult tags) use a vision-language model or a small detector. Declare it. Cross-check with rules so that no answer relies on the model alone.

3. Lines

  • Extract line segments and polylines (vector) or detected segments (raster). Merge collinear pieces.
  • Classify by stroke width and dash pattern: thick solid is a process line; thin dashed or dash-dot is a signal line. Learn thresholds per drawing from the histogram of widths.
  • Snap and split. Snap endpoints within a tolerance (for example 1.5 times the stroke width). Split lines at T-junctions. A crossing without a junction dot is not a connection; a crossing with a dot, or a T, is. Detect small filled dots.
  • Ports. Where a process line ends on an equipment boundary, create a port on that equipment.
  • Instrument attachment. A signal line from a bubble to a process line or equipment attaches the instrument there. A bubble placed directly on a line attaches at that point.

4. Graph and flow direction

Nodes   : equipment (with ports), instruments (IO and non-IO), valves, junctions, off-page connectors
Edges   : process line segments between nodes (with polyline geometry), signal links (instrument to host)
Evidence for direction of each process edge:
   arrowheads on the line (strong) ;  pump: suction -> discharge (strong) ;  check valve triangle (strong)
   off-page labels "FROM T-101" / "TO UNIT 200" (strong) ;  vessel nozzle semantics: feed in, product out (medium)
   layout prior: left-to-right and top-to-bottom (weak)
Solve   : choose orientation o(e) in {+1,-1} per edge maximising sum of evidence weights
          subject to junction consistency (a split or merge has coherent inflow and outflow) and no unexplained sinks
          (small SAT / ILP with OR-Tools; fall back to propagation from the strongest evidence)

5. Simplification

  1. Define the keep set: major equipment plus IO-list instruments found.
  2. Contract every other node (elbows, reducers, minor valves, non-IO instruments) by merging its two incident edges. Remove dead ends (vents, drains) unless they carry an IO instrument.
  3. Represent IO instruments as taps: attached to the nearest process edge or equipment, ordered by distance along the flow.
  4. Check invariants: every IO tag found is still present; connectivity between kept nodes equals the original (reachability preserved); no dangling edge.

6. Series-parallel structure

Reduce the simplified graph repeatedly: merge nodes in series, merge parallel edges, and compare parallel branches. If two branches have matching sequences of component types (pump with a PT and FT, each) and common endpoints, mark them as a duty and standby group (name suffixes A and B are a supporting hint). The result is an SP-tree: series nodes lay out left to right, parallel nodes lay out as stacked rows. Parts that are not series-parallel (recycles, bypasses) go through the general layered layout, with the recycle edge drawn as a return path above.

7. Layout

  • Layering. After removing cycles, assign layers by longest path or network simplex, so flow goes left to right.
  • Ordering. Within layers, order by the barycentre heuristic to reduce crossings; keep SP groups together as clusters.
  • Routing. Orthogonal edges with a bend budget; instruments placed as bubbles on or just above their line, in flow order; minimum spacing for readability. Use ELK (elkjs, layered algorithm with orthogonal routing) or Graphviz dot with orthogonal splines. Constrain parallel groups to align.
  • Render. SVG with a small symbol library (tank, vessel, pump, exchanger, valve, instrument bubble), tags under symbols, arrowheads. Export to PDF, PPTX shapes and PNG.

Trace-back: every element of the sketch stores references to its source geometry (path ids, bounding boxes). The review view highlights these on the original P&ID when the user hovers or clicks.

9. Synthetic P&ID generator

Because real sample files are limited, build a generator that produces vector P&IDs with known ground truth. Randomly compose process templates (tank to pump to exchanger to vessel), duty and standby pairs, bypasses and recycles; add instruments (PT, FT, LT, TT) with random IO-list membership; route lines with bends and crossovers; render with ISA-like symbols and noise (width variation, text as outlines, raster scan simulation). Output the PDF, the IO list and the ground-truth graph. Use it for unit tests, accuracy numbers on unseen drawings, and optional detector training.

Operators need a clean picture of the process, but engineers still trace every P&ID by hand. Untangle Graph reads the drawing, builds a flow graph with evidence for every connection, keeps only the equipment and IO instruments, recognises duty and standby pumps as parallel branches, and redraws it left to right with straight lines. Likely judge questions

How do you know the connections are right?

Every edge is built from traced geometry with evidence for direction; we measure edge precision and recall against ground truth and let users trace any edge back to the drawing.

Does it work on a drawing it has never seen?

The approach uses generic geometry and tag grammar, and we test on synthetic drawings with randomised layouts and on the sample set.

What about scanned drawings?

We handle them through rendering and OCR, with lower confidence flagged; vector input is the reliable path.

How are duty and standby pumps recognised?

By series-parallel reduction: two branches with matching component sequences between the same endpoints.

Where is AI used?

Symbol classification and reading difficult text where needed, and layout heuristics; all cross-checked by deterministic rules. We declare the models.

What if an IO tag is not found?

It is reported in the tag-check report with a reason (near miss, likely on another sheet) rather than invented.
