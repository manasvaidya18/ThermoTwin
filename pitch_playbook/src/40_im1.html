Understand the problem

In plain words

Developers now build software together with an AI assistant: they plan, prompt, check what comes back and fix its mistakes. Most coding tests still make candidates solve puzzles alone against a timer. iMocha is prototyping an assessment in which the candidate gets a small real-world task and a full workspace in the browser (editor, terminal, live preview, AI assistant), builds something that works, and the recruiter gets a report on the skills shown. The basics work. The open questions are the candidate experience and how useful, fair and understandable the skill report is.

What iMocha already ships (from their public help pages, read today)
  • A "Coding Assessment with AI Assistant": problem on the left, code editor in the centre, AI assistant panel on the right.
  • The assistant is hint-only: one hint, explanation or guiding question at a time; it never writes passing code. It cannot see the candidate's code unless it is attached with an @ mention.
  • Two separate results: the coding score, and an AI fluency badge (High, Medium, Low) built from three A, B, C graded parameters: Context Quality, Critical Thinking, Collaboration. Recruiters can read the full chat transcript.
  • Candidates who never use the assistant receive Low overall. Messages up to 15,000 characters; any language is accepted without penalty.
  • Listed as not in the current release: a practice or preview mode, on-request re-evaluation of grades, Excel export.

The PDF's problem is a prototype aimed at the experience and the report, so our value is in what the current product does not do. Treat this as public documentation, which may change, and present your ideas as complementary, never as criticism.

What the PDF requires

  • Candidate experience: a welcoming onboarding or practice mode, clear task guidance, and an assistant that coaches and explains rather than just handing over code.
  • Showing how the candidate worked: capture the journey (planning, prompting, testing, fixing) in a way candidates find fair and transparent.
  • A clear skill report: one-page visual summary of skills, strengths and gaps, each result explained in plain language and backed by examples from the candidate's own work.
  • AI collaboration insights: how clearly the candidate stated intent, whether they checked the AI's output, whether they understood the code it produced.
  • Feedback for candidates: what went well and what to practise next, without revealing hidden checks.
  • Outcomes: a working prototype (a mock workspace or recorded sample session is acceptable), a sample skill report generated from at least one session, and a clear explanation of why the report is fair and easy to trust. Stages: mock-ups first, then a working core flow on a sample task, then a full end-to-end experience with richer insights and fairness handling.

Our concept: VibeLens

1. Capture the whole workflow

Every prompt, edit, run, test, paste and accept or reject becomes an event. The session is segmented into Understand, Plan, Build, Verify, Fix and Reflect, so the report can talk about how the candidate worked, not just what they typed to the assistant.

2. Evidence you can replay

Every score has two or three evidence moments. Click one and the session replays at that timestamp. Recruiters can check the claim themselves in 20 seconds. Candidates see exactly why they got a result.

3. Fairness you can test

Rubric-anchored scoring, language-neutral prompt assessment, scoring agreement between independent judges, a parity test on synthetic sessions, human re-evaluation on request. A "Why you can trust this report" panel makes it visible.

Why we win

It answers every bullet in the PDF's expected solution and builds on, rather than repeats, what the sponsor's product already has. Replay-linked evidence and the fairness panel are memorable and easy to demo.

Why we might lose

This is the sponsor's headline problem and will attract the most submissions. A chat-plus-score version will blend in. Scope risk is real: a full workspace is large, so lead with the capture, replay and report, and use a recorded or mock session as the PDF explicitly allows.

How it works inside

1. Event capture

Event { t, session_id, type, payload }
types:  prompt{text, attached_code?}  ai_reply{text, code_blocks[]}  insert_ai{block_id}  paste{chars, source_guess}
        edit{file, range, inserted, deleted}  run{cmd}  test{passed, failed, names}  error{message}
        file_open{file}  focus_change{visible}  idle{seconds}  preview_load{ok}  reflect{text}
Snapshots: full code snapshot at run, test, insert and every 60 s; diffs between snapshots keep storage small
Replay:   apply events in order to rebuild editor state; a scrubber jumps to any timestamp

Typing every keystroke is not needed. Aggregate typing bursts (start, end, characters). Candidates are told exactly what is captured, in the onboarding.

2. Provenance: who wrote each line

  • Maintain the document as lines with tags: human, ai, ai-edited. When the candidate presses insert or pastes text that matches an AI code block (fuzzy match above about 0.8), tag the lines as ai. A later edit that changes more than about 20% of a line's characters turns it into ai-edited.
  • At the end compute: share of final lines by tag, AI lines removed, AI lines rewritten, and the largest unreviewed paste (inserted with no run or test within two minutes).
  • This feeds the AI Reliance Map: the final code coloured by provenance, a quick, honest picture.

3. Phases

Label each event with an intent: clarify requirements, plan approach, generate code, debug an error, explain code, test or review, refactor. Rules classify non-chat events (a test run is "verify"). A small LLM call classifies prompts, checked against rules. Then segment by change points into Understand, Plan, Build, Verify, Fix, Reflect. Render as the Session DNA ribbon: coloured segments on a time axis with markers.

5. Comprehension micro-checks

At the end, ask two short questions generated from the candidate's final code, for example "what does summarise([]) return?" or "why does this loop stop?". The answer key is computed by running the code, not by an LLM opinion, so grading is objective. Right answers show understanding of what was built, whoever wrote it. This is the cleanest measure of "did they understand the code the AI produced".

  • Map metrics to levels with transparent bands (a documented table). Qualitative judgements (such as prompt clarity) come from an LLM judge that must return JSON with level, rationale and evidence event ids. A checker rejects any evidence id that does not exist or does not match the claim.
  • Two judges (different prompts or models) score independently; agreement is shown; a gap larger than one level lowers the confidence band and flags the dimension for human review.
  • Show each dimension as a level with a confidence band, never a false-precision percentage.

Parity test in practice. Generate synthetic sessions from personas (verifier, delegator, explorer, driver) with the same behaviour but different language styles; run the pipeline; report the score differences. If they exceed tolerance, the metric or judge prompt is changed. Display "tested on N synthetic sessions" in the trust panel.

8. The assistant: a coach, not a solver

  • Modes an organisation can pick: hint-only, pair (shows small snippets), open (vibe mode). The report notes the mode so comparisons are fair.
  • Hint ladder. Level 1 nudge, level 2 concept, level 3 pseudo-steps, level 4 small snippet if the mode allows. Moving down the ladder is recorded.
  • Context. A "share my code" toggle makes it explicit when the assistant sees the candidate's code; the act of sharing is itself a context-quality signal.
  • Explain mode for errors and code; the assistant asks "what did you expect to happen?" when the prompt is vague.

9. Task design

A 30 to 45 minute task such as "expense tracker with CSV import and monthly summary". The spec has realistic gaps (negative amounts, duplicate rows, time zones) and hidden tests by category (happy path, edge cases, robustness). The assistant's natural mistakes on those edges are what trap-catch rates observe. Do not sabotage the assistant: that would be unfair and unrepeatable.

Screens and flows

  • Welcome and practice. What is measured, what is captured, a 5-minute sandbox task, a try-the-assistant moment.
  • Task screen. Task brief with acceptance criteria; editor; terminal or run output; live preview; assistant panel with hint ladder; timer; "share my code" toggle.
  • Finish. Two comprehension questions and a short reflection prompt ("what would you do next").
  • Candidate report. Friendly summary, strengths, practise-next items, link to replay of their own session.
  • Recruiter report (one page). Candidate and task header; role-readiness band; radar and dimension bars with confidence; archetype; Session DNA; three moments that mattered with replay buttons; AI collaboration insights (intent clarity, output checked, code understood); code outcome by hidden-test category; the trust panel.
  • Replay. Scrubber, event list, editor state, assistant chat.
  • Recruiter list. Candidates, filters, compare, export.

Data and evaluation

  • Sessions. Record two or three real sessions from teammates, and generate 20 to 30 synthetic persona sessions for the fairness tests. Use only consenting, non-sensitive data.
  • Metrics to report: inter-judge agreement (percentage within one level, Cohen's kappa), parity test deltas across language styles, correlation of report dimensions with hidden-test outcomes, recruiter reading test (can 5 peers answer five questions about a candidate from the one-pager in under 60 seconds), candidate usefulness rating of the feedback page.
  • Honest limits: small sample, synthetic personas are not real candidates, scores are decision support not a hiring decision.

Likely judge questions

How is this different from an AI fluency score?

A chat-based score looks at the conversation. We also capture edits, runs and tests, show provenance of the final code, test comprehension by running the code, and link every score to a replayable moment. We add practice mode and a re-evaluation path.

How do you keep the scoring fair?

Rubric-anchored levels, language-neutral intent scoring, two independent judges with agreement shown, a parity test on synthetic sessions, and human re-evaluation on request.

Can an LLM judge be trusted?

Most signals are computed by code; the judge only rates qualities, must cite real event ids, and a checker rejects unsupported claims.

What about candidates who do not use the AI?

They are not penalised. Other dimensions score fully and AI judgement is marked "chose independence".

What is captured and is it private?

Prompts, edits, runs and tests for the task, with consent and a retention limit, told to the candidate up front.

Does the assistant just give the answer?

No. It coaches with a hint ladder; the mode is configurable and recorded so comparisons stay fair.

How would a recruiter use the report?

Read the one page in a minute, open the replay for any claim they want to check, compare candidates, and share the candidate feedback.
